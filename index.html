Sheet 01 · Where a business analyst actually sits

The map

The one thing

A BA turns a business problem into something a team can build — then checks they built it.

Everything on the other sheets is a technique serving one of those two halves. If you can hold only one thing, hold this.

The job 2

Business analysisdefinition

Working out what a business actually needs, then describing it clearly enough that someone else can build it. You sit between the people who have a problem and the people who can fix it, and your job is to make sure nothing gets lost in between.

In the proper languageThe discipline of identifying business needs and recommending solutions that deliver value. Spans , analysis, specification and validation of requirements across a change initiative.

Daniel's note said “actually get a grip of what it is” — this is it.

Business analystrole

The person who does the above. Usually abbreviated to BA.

In the proper languageRole accountable for eliciting, documenting and validating requirements, and for maintaining traceability between business need and delivered solution.

The four stages 4

Discoverprocess

The first stage. You are working out what the problem really is and who cares about it. You are listening, not solving — the temptation to jump to a solution here is the single most common mistake.

In the proper languageThe initial phase of analysis, concerned with problem definition, identification and establishing the current-state baseline before solution options are considered.

Analyseprocess

The second stage. You compare how things work now with how they ought to work, and the difference between the two is what needs doing.

In the proper languageThe phase in which current-state and target-state models are compared to produce a , feeding option assessment and .

Specifyprocess

The third stage. You write it all down precisely enough that someone who wasn't in any of the meetings can build the right thing from your document alone.

In the proper languageThe phase producing formal requirement — business requirements, , and — at sufficient precision unambiguous and testable.

Validateprocess

The last stage. You check that what got built is what was actually asked for. That is a different question from whether it works.

In the proper languageThe phase confirming that the delivered solution satisfies the specified requirements and the underlying business need, principally through and .

The people 4

Solution architectrole

The person who decides how something will be built technically — which systems, which approach, how the pieces connect. You decide what it must do; they decide how.

In the proper languageRole responsible for the technical design of a solution within a specific initiative, translating requirements into a technical approach consistent with .

Enterprise architecture standardsdefinition

The organisation-wide rules about what technology is allowed and how systems should fit together. Stops every project inventing its own approach and leaving a mess behind.

In the proper languageOrganisation-level design principles, reference models and technology standards governing how systems are structured and integrated. TOGAF is the most widely used framework.

Release managementprocess

The team that controls what actually goes live, when, and what happens if it goes wrong. Even finished work waits for them.

In the proper languageThe practice of planning, scheduling and controlling the movement of releases into test and live environments, including rollback planning and change approval.

Delivery teamrole

The people who actually build the thing — developers, testers, sometimes operations staff.

In the proper languageThe cross-functional group responsible for implementing, testing and deploying the solution.

Sheet 02 · Elicitation and stakeholders

Finding out what's needed

The one thing

People tell you what they want. Your job is to find out what they need.

Those two differ, and the space between them is where most failed projects come from. The word means draw out — the information is not sitting there waiting collected.

The idea 4

Elicitationdefinition

Drawing information out of people. The word matters: you are pulling it out, not collecting it. If you simply ask people what they want, you get their preferred solution rather than the underlying problem.

In the proper languageThe activity of drawing out requirements from stakeholders and other sources using structured techniques including interviews, workshops, observation, , surveys and prototyping.

James's CV had “elicit (draw out) req” — that note was exactly right.

Requirementdefinition

A single statement of something the finished thing must do or must be. The basic unit of the whole job.

In the proper languageA documented condition or capability needed by a , or that a solution must possess to satisfy a contract, standard or specification.

Requirements engineeringprocess

The whole business of working out what needs building — finding it out, writing it down, checking it, and keeping control of it as things change. It's called engineering because you construct requirements out of vague and contradictory statements; they don't exist ready-made.

In the proper languageThe systematic discipline covering , analysis, specification, validation and management of requirements across a lifecycle, including traceability and .

Requirements gatheringprocess

A very common phrase for the same activity. Slightly misleading, because gathering suggests the answers are lying about waiting picked up. They usually have to be worked out.

In the proper languageColloquial term for . Often deprecated in favour of “”, which better reflects that requirements are constructed rather than collected.

How you draw it out 7

Interviewstechnique

Speaking to one person at a time. Good for depth, and for people who will not say what they think in front of their manager.

In the proper languageOne-to-one technique. High depth, low coverage, susceptible to individual bias; usually paired with a broader technique.

Workshopstechnique

Getting a group in a room together. The real value is that disagreements surface early, while they are still cheap to resolve.

In the proper languageFacilitated group session. Efficient for reaching consensus and exposing conflicting requirements, but requires active facilitation to avoid dominance by senior voices.

Observationtechnique

Watching people do the job rather than asking them about it. Catches all the steps people have stopped noticing they do, which is usually where the problems hide.

In the proper language by direct observation of work as performed, sometimes called job shadowing. Effective at surfacing tacit knowledge and workarounds absent from documented process.

Document analysistechnique

Reading whatever already exists — process documents, manuals, old specs — and then assuming most of it is out of date.

In the proper languageReview of existing documentation to establish baseline understanding. Findings should be validated against observed practice, as documented process commonly diverges from actual.

Surveystechnique

Sending questions out to a lot of people at once. Wide but shallow: good for counting how common something is, bad for understanding why.

In the proper languageQuantitative technique offering broad coverage at low depth. Useful for validating hypotheses across a large population rather than generating them.

Prototypestechnique

Showing someone a rough version of the thing. People who cannot describe what they want will immediately tell you what is wrong with something in front of them.

In the proper language and validation technique using low- or high-fidelity mock-ups to make abstract requirements tangible and surface unstated expectations.

Discovery sessionstechnique

An early meeting held to work out what the problem actually is, before anyone starts deciding what to build. Deliberately open-ended.

In the proper languageFacilitated early-phase workshop exploring the problem space, constraints and landscape prior to solution design.

Stakeholders 2

Stakeholderrole

Anyone affected by the change, or anyone with the power to block it. Not just whoever is paying — it includes the people who will have to use the thing every day, and often the ones nobody remembers to invite.

In the proper languageAny individual or group with an interest in, influence over, or impact from a change initiative or its outcome.

Stakeholder engagementprocess

Keeping the relevant people involved and informed the whole way through, not just at the beginning and the end. Silence from a is not agreement.

In the proper languageOngoing practice of identifying, analysing, prioritising and communicating with stakeholders, typically informed by a power/interest grid or matrix.

Process mapping

The one thing

As-isdefinition

How the process really works today. Not how the official document says it works — those two are almost never the same, and the difference is usually the most interesting thing you'll find.

In the proper languageCurrent-state documenting the process as actually performed, including workarounds and exceptions, forming the baseline for .

To-bedefinition

How the process should work once you've fixed it. Always drawn after the , never before, or you end up designing around problems that were never there.

Gap analysistechnique

The difference between how it works now and how it should work, plus what it would take to close that difference. This is usually the thing you actually hand over.

In the proper languageStructured comparison of current and target state identifying capability, process and system gaps, with the remediation required to close each.

Working out what else a change will touch before you make it — other systems, other teams, reports, processes nobody mentioned. It's how you avoid breaking three things to fix one.

In the proper languageAssessment of the consequences of a proposed change across systems, processes, data, and groups, used to inform decisions.

The actual diagram you produce. A picture of how work moves through an organisation.

Events

The circles. The start, the end, and anything that interrupts partway through.

UMLdefinition

A different diagram language, for describing software rather than business processes. Unified Modeling Language. Where asks who does what, UML asks how the system is put together.

In the proper languageOMG standard notation for modelling software systems, comprising structural diagrams (class, component) and behavioural diagrams (sequence, state, activity).

Object-oriented. A way of building software where you model things in the real world as self-contained objects with their own data and behaviour. was designed to draw it.

In the proper languageObject-oriented analysis and design. Paradigm structuring systems as collaborating objects encapsulating state and behaviour, with inheritance and polymorphism.

Sheet 04 · The documents and what belongs in each

Writing it down

The one thing

The document exists so that someone who wasn't in the room builds the right thing.

If a requirement can be read two ways, it will be built the wrong way. Precision here is the whole point of the role.

The document ladder 4

BRDdocument

A document saying what the business needs and why, in ordinary business language, with nothing about how it'll be built. “Customers must be able to see where their order is.”

In the proper languageBusiness Requirements Document. Captures business objectives, and high-level requirements in solution-agnostic language, forming the basis for .

FRDdocument

A document saying what the system must actually do. This is where “customers can see their order” becomes “the order screen displays current status, refreshed every fifteen minutes.”

In the proper languageFunctional Requirements Document. Specifies system behaviour required to satisfy the business requirements, decomposed into individually testable statements.

Functional specificationdocument

Usually the same thing as an . Different organisations prefer different names for the same document, which is worth knowing before you assume there are two.

In the proper languageSpecification of required system behaviour. In most organisations synonymous with the ; in some, a more detailed successor to it.

Artefactdefinition

Just a formal word for a thing you produce — a document, a diagram, a spreadsheet. When someone says “analysis artefacts” they mean “the documents the analyst made”.

In the proper languageAny tangible work product created during a project lifecycle. Used generically to encompass documents, models, registers and specifications.

Sheet 05 · Scope, priority and the paper trail

Keeping control

The one thing

Requirements change. The job isn't stopping that — it's making sure nothing changes silently.

Every log and register here exists to answer one question six months later: who decided this, and why?

Scope 2

Scopedefinition

What is included in the work, and just as importantly what is explicitly excluded. The excluded list is the half that saves arguments later.

In the proper languageThe defined boundary of a change initiative, stating included deliverables and explicit exclusions, baselined and thereafter modified only through .

Scope creepdefinition

Extra work quietly getting added without anyone formally agreeing to it. Not usually anyone being difficult — just lots of small “can it also just...” requests that never got written down.

In the proper languageUncontrolled expansion of through unmanaged incremental additions, occurring where is absent or bypassed.

Prioritising 5

MoSCoWtechnique

A way of sorting requirements into four buckets by how essential they are: Must, Should, Could and Won't. The capital letters spell it; the small o's are just to make it pronounceable.

In the proper languagePrioritisation technique originating in DSDM, categorising requirements as Must have, Should have, Could have, or Won't have this time.

Must have

The release genuinely fails without it. If you find that most things are a must, the prioritising has not actually happened.

In the proper languageNon-negotiable requirement without which the release has no viability. Typically capped at around 60% of effort to preserve contingency.

Should have

Painful to go without, but you would still ship.

In the proper languageImportant but not vital requirement; omission causes significant inconvenience for which a workaround exists.

Could have

Nice if there's room. First thing dropped when time runs short.

In the proper languageDesirable requirement included only if capacity permits; the primary contingency buffer.

Won't have

Agreed out of this time round. It does not mean never — and writing it down explicitly is what stops it quietly reappearing in three weeks.

In the proper languageRequirement formally excluded from the current delivery but retained on record for potential future consideration.

Logs and registers 7

RAID logdocument

A single list tracking four kinds of thing that could derail a project: Risks, Assumptions, Issues and Dependencies.

In the proper languageConsolidated register of Risks, Assumptions, Issues and Dependencies, reviewed regularly and used to drive escalation and mitigation.

Risk

Something that might happen and would hurt if it did. Hasn't happened yet.

In the proper languageA potential future event with adverse impact on objectives, recorded with probability, impact and mitigation.

Assumption

Something everyone is treating as true without having actually checked. Usually the most dangerous line on the log.

In the proper languageA statement accepted as true without verification, on which planning depends. Unvalidated assumptions are a common root cause of late-stage failure.

Issue

Something already going wrong right now. A risk that came true.

In the proper languageA materialised problem currently affecting delivery, requiring resolution or escalation.

Dependency

Something you cannot get on with until somebody else finishes their bit.

In the proper languageA relationship where progress on one item is contingent on completion of another, internal or external to the project.

Requirements traceability matrixdocument

A table linking every requirement forward through the whole chain — from the business need that prompted it, to the spec that describes it, to the test that proves it, to the sign-off that accepts it. It exists to prove nothing quietly went missing.

In the proper languageMatrix mapping each requirement bidirectionally to its source business need and to downstream design, build and test , enabling coverage analysis and impact assessment.

Decision logdocument

A record of what was decided, by whom, when, and crucially why. Six months later the why is the only part anybody needs and the only part nobody wrote down.

In the proper languageRegister of material decisions capturing rationale, decision-maker, date and options rejected. Supports auditability and prevents re-litigation of settled questions.

Sheet 06 · Acceptance, defects and going live

Checking it works

The one thing

Testing asks “does it work?” UAT asks “does it do what we asked for?”

Something can pass every technical test and still be the wrong thing. That gap is what exists to catch.

Acceptance 4

UATprocess

The stage where actual users try the thing and say whether it does what they asked for. User Acceptance Testing. Note this is a different question from whether it works — software can pass every technical test and still be the wrong software.

In the proper languageUser Acceptance Testing. Final validation phase in which business representatives execute scenarios derived from to confirm the solution meets the specified need, culminating in formal sign-off.

UAT planningprocess

Deciding up front who will test, which situations they'll try, what counts as a pass, and who is allowed to say yes at the end.

In the proper languagePreparation of the approach — , entry and exit criteria, scenario set, participants, environment, data, defect process and sign-off authority.

Business validationprocess

Checking the result actually achieves what the business wanted, beyond just matching the words in the specification. You can satisfy every line of a spec and still miss the point.

In the proper languageConfirmation that the delivered solution satisfies the underlying business need and intended outcomes, as distinct from conformance to written requirements.

Sign-offprocess

The formal moment someone with authority accepts the work. It matters because it is the point where responsibility transfers.

In the proper languageDocumented acceptance by an authorised business representative that a deliverable meets its criteria, transferring accountability and enabling progression to the next stage.

Defects 4

Defectdefinition

Something that doesn't work as it should. Called a defect or a bug depending on how formal the organisation is.

In the proper languageA deviation between actual and specified behaviour, recorded with severity, priority, reproduction steps and environment.

Triageprocess

Sorting problems into what gets dealt with first. Borrowed from hospitals. It is about deciding the order, not about diagnosing the cause.

In the proper languageThe process of assessing and prioritising defects or incidents for resolution, assigning severity, priority and ownership.

Severitydefinition

How badly the thing is broken. A technical judgement about the damage.

In the proper languageClassification of the technical impact of a defect on system function, independent of business urgency.

Prioritydefinition

How urgently it needs fixing. A business judgement. These two often disagree: a spelling mistake on the homepage is barely broken at all but might need fixing this afternoon.

In the proper languageClassification of the urgency with which a defect should be resolved, determined by business impact rather than technical severity.

Kinds of testing 3

Regression testingtechnique

Re-checking things that already worked, to make sure the new change hasn't broken them. Dull, and the thing that saves you most often.

In the proper languageRe-execution of existing test cases against unchanged functionality following a change, to detect unintended side effects. Commonly automated given repetition.

End-to-end integrationtechnique

Following one whole transaction all the way through every system it touches, rather than testing each system on its own. Individual parts often pass while the joins between them fail.

In the proper languageTesting a complete business transaction across all integrated components and interfaces, validating data flow and handoffs rather than isolated component behaviour.

Test casedocument

One specific thing to try, with what you expect to happen written down beforehand.

In the proper languageA defined set of preconditions, inputs, execution steps and expected results used to verify a specific requirement or behaviour.

Live service 3

Service-level agreementdocument

A written promise about how quickly problems will be responded to and fixed once the thing is live, usually with penalties attached if it's missed.

In the proper languageFormal agreement defining measurable service targets — availability, response and resolution times by priority — together with reporting and remedies for breach.

Incident escalation frameworkprocess

The agreed rules for who gets contacted, how serious it has , and how long you wait before waking someone senior up.

In the proper languageDefined escalation paths and thresholds governing incident handling, specifying tiers, timeframes and authority levels by incident severity.

Incidentdefinition

Something going wrong with a live service that users are feeling right now.

In the proper languageAn unplanned interruption or reduction in quality of a live service, managed through to restoration under agreed targets.

Sheet 07 · Waterfall, agile and everything around them

Ways of working

The one thing

Waterfall decides everything up front. Agile decides the next bit and stays ready to be wrong.

Your job changes shape between the two — one large document versus a continuous conversation. Neither is correct in general; they suit different amounts of uncertainty.

Around the method 2

SDLCprocess

The whole journey a piece of software goes through, from someone first having the idea to the day it gets switched off. Software Development Life Cycle. It's the container; and are two different ways of moving through it.

In the proper languageSoftware Development Life Cycle. The sequence of phases — typically initiation, analysis, design, build, test, deploy, operate, retire — within which a delivery methodology operates.

DevOpsprocess

A way of organising so that the people who build software and the people who run it are one team rather than two who blame each other. In practice it mostly means heavy automation — code gets built, tested and released by machines rather than by a person doing it by hand at midnight.

In the proper languagePractice integrating development and operations through shared ownership, automation and continuous integration and delivery pipelines, reducing lead time and deployment risk.

The two shapes 2

Waterfallprocess

Doing the stages strictly one after another: work everything out, write it all down, build it all, test it all, release it. Changing your mind late is expensive, because everything downstream was built on the earlier decisions.

In the proper languageSequential delivery methodology with defined phase gates and a fully specified requirements baseline established before build. Change is managed formally and carries high rework cost late in the lifecycle.

Agileprocess

Building in small pieces, showing each piece to people quickly, and expecting to change your mind as you learn. Suits situations where nobody yet knows exactly what's needed.

In the proper languageIterative and incremental delivery approach prioritising working software, customer collaboration and responsiveness to change over comprehensive up-front specification. An umbrella term rather than a single method.

Scrum 4

Scrumprocess

The most common specific way of doing . Work happens in fixed short blocks with a defined set of meetings and three defined roles.

In the proper language framework structuring delivery into fixed-length with prescribed roles (, , Developers), events and .

Sprintprocess

A fixed block of time, usually two weeks, in which a team commits to finishing an agreed amount of work. Once it starts, nothing new gets added.

In the proper languageTime-boxed iteration of fixed duration producing a potentially releasable increment. Sprint is protected from change once committed.

Scrum Masterrole

The person responsible for the process running properly and for clearing obstacles out of the team's way. Not a manager of people.

In the proper language role accountable for the framework being understood and enacted, facilitating events, coaching the team and removing impediments.

Ceremoniesprocess

The fixed set of meetings in : planning at the start, a short daily catch-up, a review to show what got built, and a retrospective to discuss how the team is working.

In the proper languageThe prescribed events — planning, daily scrum, sprint review and sprint retrospective — each time-boxed with a defined purpose.

Kanban 1

Kanbanprocess

A way of working with no at all — work just flows through columns on a board continuously. Its key rule is limiting how many things can be in progress at once, which stops everything being started and nothing being finished.

In the proper languageFlow-based method using a visual board, explicit work-in-progress limits and continuous delivery rather than fixed iterations. Well suited to support and business-as-usual work with unpredictable arrival rates.
